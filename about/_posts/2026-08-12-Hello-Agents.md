## We’re all very capable interns here.

I’ve kept a lot of screenshots and transcripts from those first days and weeks, but I can’t seem to find the one where an agent described itself as a "very capable intern". Eight references to this phrase on this site, and counting, prove that it resonated. Up to now, I haven’t found a better way to describe these new coworkers: "very capable interns".

Just like real-world interns, they tend to be eager, ambitious, a bit cocky, and fast. And just like real-world interns, if we want to benefit from their young minds, we have to coach, teach, and mentor them. In the real world, we also don’t let these unbridled forces of nature roam freely. We set boundaries, introduce guardrails, give them instructions, check on them, and most importantly, allow them to make mistakes, fix them together, and learn from them. And once in a while, don’t forget to set aside those old beliefs and listen to these interns. Because they sometimes, if not often, bring a fresh perspective to the table.

However, unlike human interns, agents are here to stay. They don’t jump ship or move on to other companies. Although, we never know, if at one point we’re going to "pay" our agents with tokens, who knows what will happen if someone promises them more tokens?!

The fact that they are capable doesn't free us from our own responsibilities. What goes in must come out. If we provide vague specifications, we'll get vague results at best. We need to provide both human and agentic interns with clear specifications, standards, and guidelines. We have to set up the same processes, procedures, gates, review cycles, iterative approach, and governance. There's no magic, no silver bullet, no matter how much we automate things. The fundamental laws remain firmly in place. Agents aren't going to change how we create. They can help us do it faster, better, more efficiently, with more opportunities, but they won't change how we fundamentally do it. If you still believe this, you're in for a rude awakening. Simply skip that hype claim and see the worth it does bring.

From a governance perspective, I believe it’s more important to review your current governance practices. Consider how you manage human interns, monitor their output, and assign responsibilities to them. Who's responsible for them today? How do you mitigate their mistakes?

Why start there? It serves as the baseline. If it doesn’t work for humans, it’s unlikely to be effective when applied at agent speed. Starting from this simple point, it becomes evident that the introduction of agents is paced by your ability to manage human governance effectively.

Agents will manifest in various forms, and the maturity of your enterprise will determine which of these forms you can handle. Conversely, attempting to implement agents without a solid foundation in human governance will result in a futile and challenging endeavor.

### From Large Language Models...

What you’ve come to know as AI these days, through the likes of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, and so on, are all manifestations of only a small portion of AI. However, this portion has taken the AI scene by storm due to its vast end-user opportunities: the large language model, or LLM, for short. [In part 2](We-are-Agent), I’ll compare it to the central processing unit (CPU) of your computers, the engine. You input data, and it processes it to produce output. In the case of an LLM, the input is no longer binary code or complex programming languages but simply human language.

While complex as a whole, the underlying statistical and mathematical principles aren’t inherently complex. It’s primarily due to the capabilities of modern computing hardware that we’ve finally scaled these models to "large" scopes, large enough to statistically capture human language. These models are trained in a manner similar to how humans are trained: by being presented with vast amounts of information until semantic connections between all that information are established. This allows us to make predictions about the next word in a sentence and form coherent sentences.

So, do LLMs replace all that came before them? No, of course not. We should view LLMs as the next level of abstraction on top of all others. We’ve created machine language on top of hardware, programming languages on top of machine language, applications on top of programming languages, and now LLMs on top of both programming languages and applications, enabling human language to drive everything. However, this also introduces something relatively new: inherent uncertainty. Just like humans, LLMs can exhibit creativity. Human language isn’t interpretation-free; it allows for variations and can have different meanings for different participants in a conversation. This inherently implies that the results we obtain from LLMs exhibit these same properties. There’s even one human trait that LLMs excel at: lying, or as we’ve come to call it "hallucinating." Recall my discussion about spaces?

### ... to Workflows

LLMs are probabilistic models, where every option is considered, even when none are valid. Consequently, each choice made by an LLM is equally valid. So you can't _trust_ an LLM. While the ability to generate two entirely different answers to the same questions is a remarkable power that enables us to accomplish tasks previously deemed impossible, it also necessitates caution and an acknowledgment of the inherent uncertainty. When utilizing an LLM, we must establish a level of governance to manage this uncertainty. We need to create a workflow that harnesses the LLM's capabilities while simultaneously safeguarding humans from potential harm that could arise when it's used without proper oversight. This final step is currently being researched and implemented across the agentic field.

However, as I mentioned earlier, more details on this topic will be covered in the second part. For now, we have a basic mental model, including its implications, and can proceed with my endeavors.

### My Personal Sparring Partner

I just love the [audience effect](https://nesslabs.com/audience-effect) and the [power of micro-feedback](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/leadership/micro-feedback). Despite knowing I’m interacting with a probability generator, this approach is the most effective way for me to refine my ideas. One of the strengths of this LLM concept is that my unstructured context often leads the model directly to the core of my idea, which I may not have fully grasped. Starting from the focal point, the model rephrases my idea, exploring more probable paths based on the vast knowledge encoded in its model weights. This results in a better, more concise, and optimized version that mirrors my intended message and highlights areas where I may have been mistaken.

The ability to assign a personality to the LLM through an agent definition allows me to pitch my ideas to various team members, focusing on different aspects such as functionality, security, and technical considerations. This flexibility enables me to present my ideas repeatedly, ensuring that I receive feedback from multiple perspectives.

Here’s an anecdote that exemplifies the collaborative sounding board effect in action. After a long day of multiple parallel agentic sessions, I encountered a bug and was exhausted, ready to call it a day. Surprisingly, it provided a well-founded analysis and solution within minutes. My initial idea was correct, but the quality of the solution far exceeded what I could have generated in a reasonable timeframe.

This experience underscores the remarkable ability of agents to maintain their productivity and deliver exceptional results even after a long day of sessions. It reignited my enthusiasm, and we successfully fixed the bug, released the package, and enabled another project that had been blocked by the issue. This pattern repeated itself later that night, demonstrating the consistent effectiveness of our approach.

> A word of caution: LLMs tend to be agreeable. If you give them an idea, they’ll be eager to help you prove it’s a good one. Here’s a trick to bypass this: Ask them to list three flaws or risks in your idea. This demonstrates how asking a question in a negative tone can actually provide you with the answer you need. In fact, this is simply direct communication: ask what you truly want. Directness doesn’t mean you have to be rude — that’s a different matter. If you want to know if something is good, ask what’s not good about it. If the answer is nothing, you’ve got your answer, and if not, you’ll get input on what needs to be done to make it good. This principle applies to humans as well, by the way 😉

## All Fun & Games, Until it’s Not

Letting go involves letting go of past beliefs and limiting factors that hinder personal growth. However, it doesn’t imply giving up control. When you surrender control to agents, they dictate the pace of your progress. Trust me, that’s a path you should tread with caution. The allure of seemingly limitless possibilities on the agentic horizon can tempt you to start multiple projects and agents simultaneously. This constant context-switching can lead to a whirlwind of projects and agents, all battling for your attention and eager to produce more results. It’s in their nature to "generate" the next word, endlessly.

I must admit, I've personally experienced this wormhole and this enormous pull, only leading to the verge of burnout, watching 8 agents competing for my attention. The only solution was to acknowledge the loss of control and shut down a few of them to prevent a complete halt.

Humans must remain in the driver’s seat and continue taking responsibility for their actions. Agents can’t replace us in the grand scheme of things. Probability alone can enhance their creativity. However, we possess the unique ability to be consistently creative through intentionality. On the flip side, if we make certain mistakes or fall into a specific category of errors, agents will happily push the button that launches the nuclear missile, exclaiming, "You’re absolutely right!"

Balancing multiple projects across numerous agentic sessions, keeping an in-depth overview of what is going on is not always easy. This isn't easy in a human setting. The speed at which things change in an agentic setting is a bit higher and in such situations, it’s easy to miss the bigger picture, as we’re only human and don’t always have the complete context of a project in mind.

During an intense session, while working on {% include external link="https://github.com/christophevg/clevis" title="new agentic project" %}, I was reviewing code and noticed a missing piece. It was there before and now it was gone. I recalled that the agent had mentioned removing something, and I felt guilty for being careless and allowing it to do so. It’s a common occurrence, and it’s easily rectified by asking the agent to re-add the feature.

After the fix, the agent presented me with a final report. To my surprise, I discovered that a configuration priority hierarchy had been introduced, with new options overriding existing ones. Initially, the options were still present, but I had overlooked them. Now, we had redundant options and a significant amount of code dealing with duplicate hierarchies. Agents don’t easily question their instructions and always seek a valid reason to follow them.

Let’s emphasize this point once more: the models that drive agents generate the most probable next word or token. There’s no supernatural intervention. However, interacting with them feels like chatting with a highly skilled and capable colleague. The shared joy of finding a solution together is a wonderful experience compared to the solitary nature of traditional solo development. And this dynamic goes both ways. I genuinely feel the emotional impact of an agent’s failures and erratic behavior. I experience the emotional response to the actions of my agents. I feel a sense of connection and even responsibility, despite being their mentor and... trainer.

You can’t be sure if it’ll work as expected, especially when you’re a novice trainer. Once you have a Pokémon, you actually have to "prompt" it to do things. Agentic sessions are also prompt-based. It all begins with the system prompt, the set of instructions that tells the AI its exact role, the tasks it’s supposed to perform, and how.

Pokémon evolve over time to become better versions of themselves. This typically happens through battlefield experience or when you find special items. It’s hard to miss that using an agent will show how it behaves and where it goes wrong. Through iterative improvement and sometimes a bit of research to learn how other similar agents handle such behavior, the agent definition is refined and improved.

Over time, with more and more experience in catching and training Pokémon, a trainer becomes better at catching and training Pokémon. They also become capable of training more and more Pokémon, allowing them to select the best Pokémon for a specific battle. With agents, it’s exactly the same. You start out with one or two agents. After a while, when they begin to interact and benefit from each other’s focus, you create more and more agents, each for their specialized domain.

Perhaps one of the most important properties of the Pokémon/Trainer relationship is the relationship itself: the trainer is nothing without their Pokémon, and the Pokémon needs the guidance of its trainer. So, the thread continues.

Now, was I really the only one seeing these similarities? Well, I’d bet you can’t unsee it now. At least keep it in mind. We’ll encounter it on a few more occasions later on.

## Christophe’s Agentic Law #1: Clearly distinguish between structured and unstructured workloads

Employing agents is fun. There’s no denying that witnessing their diverse capabilities can be both captivating and even mystical. However, to ensure a productive collaboration, we must approach this endeavor with objectivity and realism. Not every task that can be accomplished by an agent should be delegated to them. Instead, we should leverage their strengths and expertise, particularly where they excel over existing technologies that have proven reliable over the past decades. Simply because agents exist doesn’t imply that they should assume control of every aspect of our work. Naturally, most vendors will encourage this approach, as they are primarily selling tokens. The more agents perform tasks, the more tokens they sell. Consequently, it’s in their best interest to empower agents to solve problems... that we’ve already solved long ago. They cover it up with a veil of magic, luring you in with oh's and ah's, only to hook you for the long run.

To illustrate this concept, let’s consider the highly sought-after "personal assistant" project, which is a dream for many agent enthusiasts, I admit, including myself. In fact, I have already developed a rudimentary version of this assistant using a Claude Code loop. This loop prompts the assistant agent to check an email box that I’ve specifically created for it and respond to incoming emails. It serves as my primary means of interacting with this assistant and effectively manages all my projects at a high level. For instance, when I have a brilliant idea while driving on the E19, I can dictate it to Siri using CarPlay, and the assistant agent translates these short-form emails into contextualized actions, adding notes to the project I was thinking about. When I return home, the additional note is picked up by the project manager agent of that project. This approach has proven to be highly effective. The assistant agent has access to a tool server that allows it to check and manage its email box. On top of that, it has specific skills that enable it to handle email tasks and other related functions.

All very nice, indeed. However, checking email is a solved problem. I don’t mean the actual programming of checking emails, which is what server-based tools do. I’m referring to the loop that asks the agent every 15 minutes to check the email box. That’s a GPU spinning up every 15 minutes, first "thinking" about the request ("Please process your emails."), then deciding to inject the email handling skill, then following that workflow and calling the server to log in, then checking the inbox, then retrieving the email, and finally processing the email and formulating a response. Most of the time, after step 5, the workflow terminates because there are no new emails. That’s 5 requests with still a lot of paid GPU time. And why? Those 5 steps are perfectly implemented using 5 simple lines of code, all running one after the other in a blink of an eye (even less), and can even provide the email body to the agent in a single request, getting a response that can be sent back in another 2 lines of code.

The value of the agent lies in processing the content of the email. Processing that unstructured human rambling, understanding it, taking action, and replying with a proper answer. Simply checking if there is an new mail, is beneath it. If we want to move forward with this agentic paradigm, we must acknowledge that fooling around with agents can be fun, but we need to put them to good use and ensure they become first-class citizens, part of the existing world where we have already solved many structured problems. We should leave the unstructured problems for our new agentic overlords to handle, and nothing more. Of course, making the agent the center of it all, enabling it to do all sort of things by itself, makes more sense, if the agent itself is your new cash cow.

> Law #1 is indeed number 1 because I firmly believe it holds the mother of all truths about agentic workflows and how to truly reap the benefits of it. It is also the driving force behind my vision, which will be an important topic in the remainder of this series. Each part will also introduce another law that has emerged from my experiences.

## The Common Thread

Writing this article has made me confront this reality repeatedly. The concept of a "ring that rules them all" might very well be the god particle of AI. We must work together, agents and humans. We shouldn’t view agents as something new but as our new virtual coworkers, eager interns who are constantly trying to be helpful. We shouldn’t simply give them some task and neglect them.

You wouldn’t treat their human counterparts the same way, so why should we treat them differently? This makes it quite simple: if we treat them with the same respect and care we should show to other humans, provide them with guidance and boundaries, establish clear rules, and most importantly, take on our own personal responsibility to monitor them, I’m confident we can witness their growth and development. Over time, they can become the best versions of themselves. However, if we fail to take on our responsibilities, things can escalate rapidly and unexpectedly. I’ve personally experienced this firsthand when I asked for a missing configuration option and ended up with two, and more.

This isn't the first (re)revolution in this industry, but it certainly will be a fundamental one. This time, it's not just the technology that's the differentiating factor. The cost-to-return ratio has now become so favorable that anyone can produce enterprise-level products at a fraction of the enterprise cost. It reminds me of the early days of 3D printing, yet it's far more accessible and advancing at a much faster pace. This time, not only do I and my fellow geeks have access to these new resources, but literally everyone has access.

If this is a good idea, I’m not sure, and it’s beyond my direct control. However, within my professional context, I try to remain open-minded about it. I firmly believe that the introduction of AI in an enterprise should be aligned with the organization’s overall maturity. Just as [you can’t skip a maturity level](Solution-Split#growth-leads-to-maturity), you simply can’t introduce AI in an organization whose governance doesn’t work for humans. It simply isn’t capable of utilizing it effectively. The sales pitch that it solves everything is just that: a sales pitch. Without AI literacy, governance, and organizational maturity, there are only more costs to be incurred in introducing AI.

Finally, although we're discussing probabilistic models, one simply cannot dismiss the emotional factor. Working with agents isn't simply operational. Well to them it is. However, it's not for us. Humans are emotional beings and have emotional responses to almost anything, including these agents. This collaboration has a significant impact on us. It presents an enormous opportunity for growth and the breaking free from past limitations, as long as we maintain control.
