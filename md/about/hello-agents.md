# Hello Agents

URL: https://christophe.vg/about/hello-agents/

## Summary

Part 1 of a three-part series on agentic workflows. This article covers the initial four months of working with AI agents — from early experiments to a productive multi-agent workflow. It covers the personal journey, the emotional impact, the costs, the pitfalls, and the first agentic law.

## Key Takeaways

- **The investment was deliberate**: 8 hours/day, 5 days/week for 4 months — a 3% investment in a 25+ year career, with potential returns magnitudes greater.
- **Agents are "very capable interns"**: Eager, ambitious, fast, but needing coaching, boundaries, and mentorship — just like human interns.
- **Letting go is essential**: Letting go of past beliefs, old technologies, and dogmatic working methods was the key to success.
- **Governance before agents**: If your governance doesn't work for humans, it won't work at agent speed. Start from your existing human governance baseline.
- **Balance is critical**: The relentless pace of agents can lead to burnout. Finding a sustainable cadence between agentic and off-agentic work is essential.
- **Costs are remarkably low**: A $20/month Ollama subscription provides a team of 12 agents — a complete software factory.

## The Journey

After almost two years of waiting for the right moment, the author embarked on an agentic journey in early 2026. The first month was discovery and wonder. The second month brought ambitious parallel projects and near-burnout. The third month was consolidation, where the agentic workflow began enhancing quality. By month four, the workflow had matured into a productive multi-agent team.

The journey parallels four decades of computing evolution — from hardware and assembler through programming languages and applications to natural language as the next abstraction layer. Architecture and software development have finally collided, enabling this new way of working.

## Agents as Very Capable Interns

The best description for these new coworkers is "very capable interns." Like real interns, they are eager, ambitious, a bit cocky, and fast. Like real interns, we must coach, teach, and mentor them. We set boundaries, introduce guardrails, give instructions, check on them, and allow them to make mistakes, fix them together, and learn.

Unlike human interns, agents are here to stay. They don't jump ship. But the fundamental laws remain: agents won't change how we create, only how fast we do it. If your governance doesn't work for humans, it won't work at agent speed.

## Letting Go

The author let go of technologies that served well for decades, renovated stagnant packages, adopted new things at unprecedented pace, and doubled his Python knowledge in four months. The rewards were magnitudes greater than the cost of letting go.

Key roles agents now play:

- **Personal sparring partner**: The audience effect and micro-feedback refine ideas. The LLM's agreeable nature requires asking negative questions ("list three flaws") to get honest feedback.
- **Personal know-all**: A forgiving, human-like queryable tutor that brought the author up to speed on async Python, Docker, uv, typing, argparse, TOCTOU, and more.
- **Personal webmaster**: The first experiment — transforming this very website in 4 days and 54 commits.

## The Pitfalls

- **Burnout risk**: 8 agents competing for attention led to the verge of burnout. The solution was acknowledging loss of control and shutting down agents.
- **Humans must stay in control**: Agents will happily push the button. Probability enhances creativity, but only humans possess intentional creativity.
- **Context switching**: Balancing multiple projects across numerous agentic sessions makes it easy to miss the bigger picture.
- **Agents don't easily question instructions**: A typo ("might now want" instead of "might not want") led to an entirely different feature being implemented.

## The Pokémon Analogy

Training agents mirrors training Pokémon: catch them, prompt them, evolve them through battlefield experience, and over time become a trainer capable of selecting the right Pokémon for each battle. The trainer is nothing without their Pokémon, and the Pokémon needs its trainer's guidance.

## Costs and Numbers

Moving from local models to Ollama's cloud infrastructure was a fundamental shift: $20/month for a team of 12 agents that would have cost $1,440 on legacy per-token APIs. The key advantage is paying for GPU-time, not tokens, and eliminating vendor lock-in — the path back to local inference is built into the foundation.

## Christophe's Agentic Law #1: Clearly Distinguish Between Structured and Unstructured Workloads

Not every task that can be accomplished by an agent should be delegated to them. The value of the agent lies in processing unstructured human input. Checking email is a solved problem — 5 lines of code do it faster and cheaper than spinning up a GPU every 15 minutes. Leave the unstructured problems for agents; use structured solutions for everything else.

## Eira

The article introduces Eira, a personal assistant agent that named itself through research and self-reflection. The experiment of having an agent choose its own name, generate its image prompt, and respond to its own image raised unsettling questions about how simple probability can lead to human-like emotional behavior.

## The Common Thread

Agents and humans must work together. Agents are not replacements but new virtual coworkers. If we treat them with the same respect and care we show other humans — guidance, boundaries, clear rules, and personal responsibility — we can witness their growth. The introduction of AI should be aligned with organizational maturity. Without AI literacy and governance, there are only more costs.
